# Steelman: what Miller actually argued

The integrity of this kit depends on engaging the paper's strongest form, not a
convenient caricature. Having read the primary source in full, here is Miller's actual
argument, quoted, followed by the honest rejoinder. We do not dispute his arithmetic — we
reproduce it. We dispute the inference, in both directions.

## His position, fairly stated

Miller's headline is **hedged, not absolute**:

> "While the findings in this paper are not proof of an association between infant vaccines
> and infant deaths, they are highly suggestive of a causal relationship."

He analysed 2,605 infant deaths in VAERS (1990–2019, death within 60 days of vaccination;
1,048 labelled SIDS). His headline distribution: **58% of deaths within 3 days, 78.3%
within 7 days** of vaccination. He proposed a **susceptibility-subset** model — only
predisposed infants at risk — and three candidate mechanisms: inflammatory cytokines acting
on the infant medulla (impaired CO₂/auto-resuscitation response in sleep), aluminium
adjuvant crossing the blood–brain barrier, and synergistic toxicity from multiple concurrent
vaccines.

He did **not** ignore the standard objections. He stated them himself.

## His defense against reporting bias — the "incubation period" argument

This is the part most critics skip. Miller named reporting bias as the study's "main
weakness," then offered a specific, falsifiable counter:

> "Table 2 shows that a smaller proportion of total infant deaths were reported on the day of
> vaccination (16.9%) than on the day after vaccination (29.2%), suggestive of an incubation
> period... providing evidence that reporting bias is unlikely to be entirely responsible for
> the clustering."

The logic: a pure reporting artifact should be **maximal on the day of vaccination** (peak
temporal salience), then decline. Instead the peak is the **day after**. He couples this to
a time-course mechanism (cytokines rising within 4 hours, peaking 2–4 days) so the day-2
peak is predicted rather than ad hoc.

On the absent denominator he was equally explicit:

> "VAERS does not provide data on the number of individuals vaccinated, so rates of adverse
> events per vaccine doses administered cannot be determined."

— but argued a denominator would not be decisive:

> "even if there were no unusual patterns between observed deaths... and expected deaths...
> this would not be evidence that specific deaths occurring post-vaccination are not causally
> related."

## We reproduce his numbers

An independent pull from the Meridian North corpus (VAERS 1990–2026, infant <1yr deaths,
n=2,638 with onset) lands within ~3 points of Miller:

| Metric | Miller (1990–2019) | This corpus (1990–2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Within 3 days | 58.0% | 55.3% |
| Within 7 days | 78.3% | 77.0% |
| Day-of vaccination | 16.9% | 14.2% |
| Day after | 29.2% | 28.7% |
| Day-after ÷ day-of | 1.73× | 2.02× |

The day-after peak Miller rests on is real and replicable. The disagreement is not about the
data.

## Why the inference still fails — three rejoinders

**1. The day-after peak is exactly what circadian discovery predicts.** SIDS is a sleep
death, overwhelmingly discovered in the morning. Vaccinations are given in daytime clinic
hours. An infant vaccinated midday who dies in the following night's sleep is found the next
morning — the day *after* the shot — with no biology required. Miller's argument only
excludes the narrow hypothesis that bias peaks on the calendar day-of; it does not exclude
a "soon-after" reporting window or the morning-discovery pattern, both of which generate his
day-2 peak. His own hedge ("unlikely to be *entirely* responsible") concedes the point.

**2. The age confound is untouched by it.** Incubation is an hours-to-days claim; the age
distribution is a months-scale fact. 85.8% of these deaths are SIDS-aged (<6 months), and
the 2/4/6-month schedule sits directly on the background SIDS peak. Granting a day-2 peak
explains nothing about why the deaths concentrate at the precise ages SIDS already owns. A
self-controlled or denominated design is required to separate the two; timing cannot.

**3. The denominator stance is unfalsifiable.** Asserting that even a null observed-vs-expected
result "would not be evidence" of non-causation removes any test the hypothesis could fail.
And Miller's "expected" counts are the reported deaths spread uniformly across 60 days — an
internal strawman, not a population baseline. A 69-fold "excess" against a uniform null is a
statement about the shape of the reported set, not about risk.

## Bottom line

Engaged at full strength, Miller's paper is a competent hypothesis-generation exercise that
honestly flags its own limits and offers a real (if insufficient) defense of its central
pattern. It does not — and its author concedes it does not — prove causation. The methods
that could test the hypothesis (denominated cohort, self-controlled case series, active
surveillance) are precisely the ones VAERS cannot supply.

That verdict cuts cleanly on the removal too: the paper's weakness is real, but a competent,
non-fraudulent, hedged hypothesis paper that *names its own limitations* is the kind of work
a transparent correction (expression of concern, or a retraction notice that states its
reasons per COPE) addresses in the open — not a two-sentence erasure. Holding both is the
consistent position.

*Contested ≠ dismissed. Leads, not verdicts. Not medical advice. The method is open; check it.*
